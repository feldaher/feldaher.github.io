Repairing the central nervous system is a fundamentally physical problem: after a traumatic injury, many cell types — neurons, astrocytes, macrophages, neutrophils — must coordinate to restore the tissue. Working with zebrafish larvae in the lab of Catherina and Thomas Becker, I combined long-term *in vivo* imaging, controlled laser lesions at cellular resolution, CRISPR genome editing and mathematical modelling to follow this process.

The main finding: **microglia are essential for brain repair, not because they trigger neurogenesis, but because they exert local traction forces on neighbouring astrocytes.** This drives a large-scale contraction of the tissue that brings surviving neurons back into a configuration where they can re-form a functional visual circuit — challenging the dominant model of repair.

Along the way I developed tools now used by others: a semi-automated laser-lesion protocol adopted by the UK Zebrafish Screening Facility, an observation chamber for long-term imaging on upright microscopes (Wellcome Trust iTPA Translational Innovation Prize, 2020), and a microcontroller-based visual stimulation system.
